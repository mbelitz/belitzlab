        Our lab is motivated by the dual crises of biodiversity loss and climate change. We harness the rapid growth of environmental data — from museum collections to community science to computer vision — to understand how insects respond to global change, and to translate that understanding into conservation and climate adaptation practice.

        ### Phenology & phenotypic responses to climate change
        We integrate observational datasets with fine-resolution climate data to quantify how insect phenology and body size respond to changing climates, and how those responses are conditioned by species traits. Current work uses computer vision to measure body size across tens of thousands of ground beetles, linking phenotypic plasticity to population and community trends.

        ### Urban ecology
        We study how urbanization reshapes insect communities across life stages, using custom-built light and frass traps paired with computer-vision identification pipelines to quantify the effects of urban development on moth abundance, diversity, and phenology.

        ### Biodiversity informatics & AI-powered monitoring
        We develop statistical methods, open-source software, and machine-learning pipelines that make natural history collections and community science data (e.g., iNaturalist, eBird) more useful for ecological research — including tools for correcting bias in presence-only data and semi-automated, AI-assisted insect monitoring networks.

        ### Assemblage-level conservation & climate adaptation
        We advocate for conservation and management approaches that act at the level of the ecological assemblage — groups of taxonomically related or functionally similar species — to bridge species- and ecosystem-scale conservation and better inform climate adaptation strategies, particularly for butterflies and other insects facing land-use and extreme-weather pressures.
